**Corrections to the setup as described in the brief.** These were checked by the coordinator in the code and are used throughout this report.

- **Assignment.** The "coverage" policy draws a new assignment every round, with the same slice in every layer. Every expert has at least one holder. In a given round an expert has 1 holder 29.6% of the time, 2 holders 44.5%, 3 holders 22.2% and 4 holders 3.7% (mean 2), from simulating the assignment code over 100,000 draws. So in the partial and sliced arms an expert is not always averaged over exactly 2 workers, and in about 30% of (expert, round) pairs it is trained by one worker and not averaged at all. The "rolling" policy, not used in Task B, would give exactly 2 holders.
- **Schedule and optimizer.** The learning-rate schedule runs once over each worker's whole 5,000-step trajectory: 100 warm-up steps in round 0 only, constant 1e-3, then linear decay to 1e-4 over the last 20%. AdamW's moments are reset every round, that is every 125 steps. So every reset after round 0 starts at the full learning rate with no warm-up, until the decay phase begins.
- **Equal compute.** The centralized run takes 20,000 sequential steps at batch 32. Each worker takes 5,000 steps at batch 32. Stage 0 has no data-parallel baseline at batch 128 and no single-worker 5,000-step baseline.
- **SPES.** Its released code never resets AdamW between rounds (note 04a), unlike ours.
- **Parameter counts.** Our experts are SwiGLU with three bias-free matrices (`cassandra_chorus/model/layers.py`). Expert parameters are 42,467,328 of 46,050,432 (92%; the total is from RESULTS.md). About 14.2M parameters are active per token: the 3.58M non-expert parameters plus 2 of 8 experts. A partial-arm worker trains about 54% of the parameters: the 3.58M non-expert parameters plus 4 of 8 experts, 24.8M. The notes' earlier figures (about 10M active, about 28M expert parameters, about 70% trainable, about 61% expert share) came from a coordinator estimate that wrongly assumed two-matrix experts. They are corrected throughout this report.

## 1. Summary

Stage 0's sliced arm ended **+10.8%** in test bits per character behind centralized training: local averaging +6.1%, partial updates +1.4%, masked routing +3.2%. No paper found in this search trains our exact configuration, so none predicts these numbers. The literature does explain the direction of each cost.

**Local averaging, +6.1% (paid by every arm).** Most likely a stack of handicaps, each measured separately:

1. **The hardest baseline in this literature**, with 4× more sequential steps. In the one paper with both baseline types, an 8×-more-steps run beat 8×-batch data parallel by 1.44% in loss and DiLoCo by 0.75% (inference from DiLoCo, Table 2, v3).
2. **Plain averaging.** At 150M, 4 workers, H = 50, it trails a Nesterov outer step at lr 1.0 by about 2.9% (inference from Khaled et al., Table 3, v2).
3. **A small model.** Tuned DiLoCo's 4-worker gap falls from +2.0% at 35M to +0.3% at 2.4B (DiLoCo scaling laws, Table 4, v1); we have about 14M active parameters.
4. **H = 125.** The gap grows with H in every sweep, for example +2.6% at H = 30 and +3.7% at H = 100 at 35M (inference from Streaming DiLoCo, Tables 5 and 6, v1).
5. **A fresh AdamW every round.** Mixed evidence: costly at a 16-step sync period, neutral at 256 (DES-LOC, Fig. 5 legend, v1).

**Confidence: moderate** on the direction and most of the size; **low** on how the 6.1 points divide.

**Partial updates, +1.4%.** Ordinary in size for language models, though its sign is not robust. A 21M Transformer lost +1.3% with 40% trainable (inference from Ro et al., Table 4, v2); a 1.3B model with local steps ranged from −1.6% to +4.3% as slices shrank (inference from Filippova et al., Table 1, v1; 03). Partial updates after a full forward pass are unbiased per step but add variance as fewer workers average a parameter (SDP, Theorem 3.1, v5); our coverage policy leaves an expert with one holder 29.6% of the time. SPES, our partial arm at 1B, reports no cost on a benchmark average too coarse to see 1 to 2% (Table 1, v3; inference, 04a). **Confidence: moderate** that +1.4% is in range; **low** on its mechanism.

**Masked routing, +3.2%.** **No paper isolates masked routing under periodic averaging.** FoMoE, the one method found with our storage, masking and holder-only averaging, changes partial updates and masking together: 2 holders of 4 cost +1.6%, +2.9% and +2.1% in cross-entropy against full replication at K = 32, 64 and 128 (inference from Table 6, v2), against our +4.4% for sliced against full averaging (inference). It differs in gating, optimizer state and expert size, and reports no centralized number. Three candidate explanations, none tested in our setting:

- **Inconsistent routing disperses training.** A router redrawn every step loses most of the MoE gain; a frozen one keeps it (Fan et al., Table 3, v1). Random routing masks cost 1.0%, 2.1% and 7.7% of BLEU at 25%, 50% and 75% masked (inference from MoEC, Table 3, v1).
- **IID data give experts trained apart nothing to specialize on.** Random-split experts were 3.0% to 5.3% worse than dense at every scale (inference from Branch-Train-Merge, Tables 1 and 4, v1), matching Task A's generalists.
- **A per-worker balance loss over 4 held experts** narrows the balance scope, pushing routing toward uniform (Demons in the Detail, Tables 1 to 3, v2); Task A had none (02a), so this is not the whole story.

**Confidence: low.**

**Context.** At 10M to 20M active parameters MoEs did not beat dense models (Slicing and Dicing, §3.1, v1). Our centralized 1.44 bits per character is plausible for 1.8 passes scored in 256-character windows; no run at our budget was found. Tuned local averaging meets the 5% allowance at 35M with 4 workers (+2.0%); our plain arm does not.

### 1. DiLoCo: DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models (2311.08105)

- **Citation:** Douillard, Feng, Rusu et al., 2023, preprint (no venue on the arXiv record or in OpenAlex). arXiv 2311.08105: v1 14 Nov 2023, v2 2 Dec 2023, v3 23 Sep 2024; read v3. https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08105
- **Read status:** full text (main body and appendix; figures not readable) [01]; sections read: §3 setup, Tables 2-4, the opening of the outer-optimizer paragraph [05].
- **What it did:** Dense Chinchilla-style transformers of 60M, 150M and 400M on C4 (subword tokens), sequence 1,024, batch 512, 88,000 total steps. By default it starts from a model pretrained for 24,000 steps; then 8 workers run H = 500 inner AdamW steps (lr 4e-4, weight decay 0.1) per round, followed by an outer Nesterov step (lr 0.7, momentum 0.9). Data shards are non-IID by default (k-means), IID also tested. Each replica keeps its Adam state. Pretraining.
- **Key findings:**
  - Table 2 (150M, 8 workers, H = 500, non-IID), perplexity: one worker 16.23; data parallel at 8× batch 15.30; 8× more sequential updates at the same batch 14.72; DiLoCo 15.02. In loss, DiLoCo is +0.75% behind the 8×-updates run and −0.68% ahead of 8×-batch data parallel; the 8×-batch run is +1.44% behind 8× updates (inference, 01).
  - §5: the outer updates "have effectively too large a batch size".
  - §3.1 and Fig. 6 (figure only): outer SGD, which is FedAvg, and outer Adam "performed poorly".
  - §3.1 and Fig. 4: at H = 1,000 perplexity rises "by only 2.9% relative to H = 50" (non-IID, pretrained start).
  - Supplementary §6.1: synchronizing the inner Adam states "did not lead to significant improvements".
  - Fig. 3: starting from random initialization gives "minimal (-0.1 PPL) degradation".
- **Bears on:** Q1 (the size of the averaging cost and what our baseline means), Q5 (outer optimizer, H, optimizer state), Q6.
- **How it differs from us:** dense, subword, 1.3× to 9× our 46M total parameters (inference), pretrained start, Adam state kept, Nesterov outer step, 8 workers, H = 500, non-IID shards.
- **A question it raises for us:** Table 2's 8×-updates row is the published analogue of our centralized arm. How much of our +6.1% would remain against a batch-128 data-parallel run of 5,000 steps, which Stage 0 does not have?

### 2. SPES: Pretraining A Large Language Model using Distributed GPUs: A Memory-Efficient Decentralized Paradigm (2602.11543)

- **Citation:** Zhang, Xiao, Wu et al., 2026, preprint (the v3 PDF says "Preprint. June 16, 2026"). arXiv 2602.11543: v1 12 Feb 2026, v2 4 May 2026, v3 15 Jun 2026; read v3, with v1 and v2 diffed against it. https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11543. Code: github.com/zjr2000/SPES, Apache-2.0.
- **Read status:** v3: full text, PDF plus HTML for the tables; v1 and v2: diffed word by word against v3; v1 sections read directly: §3.2 to §4.2, Table 1, Table 4, Appendices B and E; code files read in full or in part [04a].
- **What it did:** Decoder-only MoE pretraining (OLMo codebase, dropless, softmax routing). The controlled comparison is at 1B (0.3B active, 1.1B total, 16 experts, top-2) on SlimPajama with 50B tokens (v3; v1 and v2 say 100B). Larger runs: 2B on 16 nodes with one expert each, 7B on 4 nodes, and 9B upcycled. H = 50 at 1B, 100 for the larger runs. Each node's data are randomly sampled from the whole set (IID). Each node stores the full model, trains the shared part plus a disjoint block of owned experts, and routes over all experts, including frozen copies of the ones it does not own. At the merge, every shared parameter, router included, is a plain mean over all nodes, and each expert is copied from its one owner (code). No outer optimizer. AdamW state persists across rounds (code). Losses: balance 0.01, router z-loss 0.001 (v3 App. B). An expert-merging warm-up pulls each expert toward its most similar experts early in training.
- **Key findings:**
  - Table 1 (v3; 1B, 50B tokens; 8-task zero- or few-shot average; single runs): centralized 50.02, DiLoCo 49.79, SPES 50.95. In v1 and v2 (stated as 100B tokens): 49.7, 50.5, 51.0. No loss or perplexity is reported anywhere.
  - Table 4 (v3): without the merging warm-up 48.99, with it 50.95 (v1 and v2: 50.5 and 51.3).
  - Table A5 (100B tokens): 2, 4 and 8 nodes give 50.6, 49.7 and 49.5.
  - App. E, Fig. A2 (figure only): quality declines as H rises from 50 to 200 or 400.
  - App. A: in its convergence bound, expert blocks get no cross-node variance reduction.
- **Bears on:** Q4 (our partial-update arm at scale), Q3, Q1, Q5 (warm-up, z-loss, H, optimizer state).
- **How it differs from us:** 1.1B and up, subword; 16 experts with one owner each (ours: 1 to 4 holders per round); H = 50; AdamW kept (ours: fresh each round); z-loss and warm-up; benchmark accuracy instead of loss. Several of its benchmarks sit near chance, so a 1 to 2% loss gap would not show (inference, 04a).
- **A question it raises for us:** Does our partial arm's +1.4% shrink if AdamW state is kept across rounds, as in SPES's code, or with H = 50?

### 3. FoMoE: Breaking the Full-Replica Barrier with a Federation of MoEs (2606.19025)

- **Citation:** Sani, Cao, Kurmanji et al., 2026, preprint. arXiv 2606.19025: v1 17 Jun 2026, v2 20 Jun 2026; read v2, with all v1 table cells compared. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19025. No code linked in either version.
- **Read status:** full text (v2 PDF), Tables 1 and 3 to 6 re-read from the v2 HTML [04a]; full text including appendices A to G [04b]; v2 full text from the PDF, v1 abstract, §5 and §6.1 to §6.3, all v1 table cells compared with v2 by script [06].
- **What it did:** Cross-datacenter MoE pretraining with M = 2, 4 or 8 workers, IID partitions and K = 32, 64 or 128 local steps per round (20, 10 or 5 rounds; inference: 640 optimizer batches ÷ K, Table 7). Each expert lives on O_e workers; each worker holds the dense part plus O_e/M of the experts. In the main mode the router is partitioned with the experts, so tokens are routed only to resident experts. Dense parts are averaged over all workers; experts and their router rows within each expert's replica group. The outer step is DiLoCo-style Nesterov (0.7, 0.9) or LocalAdamW, which also averages both AdamW moments. Local optimizer state is kept across rounds. Gating is sigmoid. Trained quality runs use only a ~54M proxy (4 layers, width 256, 8 experts of hidden size 128, top-2) with a tied 200,019-token vocabulary, so about 51M of its 54M parameters are embeddings (inference, 04a and 06). About 1.34B tokens; evaluated by C4 validation perplexity. Pretraining.
- **Key findings:**
  - Table 6 (v2; cells identical to v1's Table 7), M = 4, LocalAdamW, fixed placement, perplexity for O_e = 1, 2 and 4 (full replica): K = 32: 121.42, 112.00, 103.91; K = 64: 130.79, 119.53, 104.47; K = 128: 143.72, 123.50, 111.89.
  - O_e = 2 against full replica: +1.6%, +2.9% and +2.1% in cross-entropy at K = 32, 64 and 128; O_e = 1: +3.4%, +4.8%, +5.3%. Random placement with O_e = 2: +1.9%, +2.0%, +2.4% (inference, 06; reshuffle period not listed, section 7).
  - Same table: at M = 8 and K = 128, partial replication beats full replication (O_e = 4: 138.88 against 159.71).
  - Tables 4 and 5: the Nesterov outer step degrades badly at K = 128 (M = 4: 420.49) where LocalAdamW does not (111.89).
  - There is no numeric centralized result in either version, and v2 softened v1's claim of matching centralized quality (§6.1). The paper calls its "±" values per-dataset spreads in §5 and per-seed spreads in App. F, so whether they are seed spreads is unresolved (section 7).
- **Bears on:** Q4 (the closest method found), Q2 and Q3 together, Q1 (outer step, kept state), Q5 (overlap, placement).
- **How it differs from us:** sigmoid gating chosen so that a missing expert "must not skew the probability distribution" of the rest (App. B.3.2), with no renormalization; experts are about 4% of its parameters, assuming two matrices per expert (inference, 06), against about 92% of ours (inference: SwiGLU experts, 42.5M of 46.05M); kept or averaged optimizer state; 5 to 20 rounds (inference); subword tokens; no centralized anchor.
- **A question it raises for us:** With Nesterov outer momentum (implemented in our harness) and kept AdamW state, would our sliced-against-full gap of +4.4% fall toward FoMoE's 2%? How much does our softmax renormalization over held experts add?

### 4. Outer optimizers: Understanding Outer Optimizers in Local SGD: Learning Rates, Momentum, and Acceleration (2509.10439)

- **Citation:** Khaled, Kale, Douillard et al., 2025, preprint (venue: see section 7). arXiv 2509.10439: v1 12 Sep 2025, v2 11 Dec 2025; read v2 (PDF, plus arXiv HTML v2 for Tables 3 to 5). https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10439
- **Read status:** sections read: abstract, §1 (contributions), §4.2 (transformer pretraining), Appendix A.1.1-A.1.6, Tables 1-5; the theory sections (§2-§3 and the appendix proofs) were not read beyond the contribution statements [01].
- **What it did:** Chinchilla-style decoders of 150M, 400M and 1B on C4 from scratch; batch 512 per replica × 1,024 tokens; cosine inner schedule; inner Adam state passed from round to round. M = 4 replicas; H from 50 to 2,000, about 480 to 12 rounds at 150M (inference). Outer steps: SGD at lr 1 ("equivalent to simple averaging"), Nesterov, Schedule-Free SGD. Baselines: data parallel at the per-replica batch (1/M of the compute, a quarter here) and at 4× batch (equal FLOPs, same steps).
- **Key findings (Table 3, v2, 150M, M = 4, perplexity):**
  - Data parallel 1× batch 18.07; 4× batch 16.89.
  - Plain averaging at H = 50, 150, 250, 500, 1,000, 2,000: 17.75, 17.58, 18.20, 18.44, 18.18, 18.11. In loss that is +1.8%, +1.4%, +2.6%, +3.1%, +2.6%, +2.5% behind 4×-batch data parallel, and from H = 250 no better than one worker at a quarter of the compute (inference, 01).
  - Nesterov at outer lr 1.0: 16.38, 16.79, 17.12, 18.15, 18.75, 18.36. Nesterov at lr 0.7 is better than plain averaging at some H and worse at others.
  - Schedule-Free SGD at outer lr 2.0: 16.88 at H = 50 to 17.59 at H = 2,000 (17.18 at H = 500).
  - A.1.2: averaging is "relatively constant" from H = 250 to 2,000; Nesterov with a high outer lr is "particularly unstable". A.1.5: a constant outer lr works best and outer warm-up was "always found to be harmful".
  - Theory (abstract): the outer lr "should sometimes be set to values greater than 1".
- **Bears on:** Q1, Q5.
- **How it differs from us:** Adam state kept; cosine schedule; 150M dense subword model; same-step data-parallel baseline, which is easier than ours; no seeds reported, so these look like single runs (01).
- **A question it raises for us:** Their plain averaging at H = 500 (about 48 rounds, inference) costs +3.1% with Adam state kept and an easier baseline. How much of our further 3 points (inference) comes from the AdamW reset, our more-steps baseline and our smaller model?

### 7. Partial updates: Partial Parameter Updates for Efficient Distributed Training (2509.22418)

- **Citation:** Filippova, Katharopoulos, Grangier, Collobert, 2025, preprint (the PDF names no venue; see section 7). arXiv 2509.22418: v1 26 Sep 2025 only; read v1 (PDF, plus HTML v1 for Fig. 4b). https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22418

- **A question it raises for us:** Is our +1.4% set by the number of holders (about 2, like their 1/16 row at +4.3%), or by the trainable fraction and holder share? By trainable fraction (about 54%) we are close to their 1/4 row (about 52%, −0.1%); by holder share (one half) we match their 1/2 row (−1.6%) (fractions are inferences; header and 03).

### 8. SDP: Model Parallelism With Subnetwork Data Parallelism (2507.09029)

- **Citation:** Singh, Khalid, Cagnasso et al., 2025-2026, preprint. arXiv 2507.09029: v1 11 Jul 2025, v2 1 Oct 2025, v3 2 Oct 2025, v4 3 Oct 2025, v5 31 May 2026; read v5. https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09029
- **Read status:** full text of v5, including Appendices A to J [03].
- **What it did:** LLaMA-style pretraining on FineWeb at 134M, 500M and 1B with 8 workers, plus ResNet and Swin on CIFAR. Masks are fixed and overlap across workers. Gradients are averaged over holders every step, so there are no local steps. Runs are FLOP-matched: sub-network runs take more steps. Two regimes: backward masking (full forward pass, masked update; the analogue of our partial arm) and forward masking (parameters dropped from the forward pass too; the analogue of our sliced arm).
- **Key findings:** At 1B (Table 1, v5) with coverage C = 0.69: data parallel 2.452; block forward-masked (B-SDP) 2.437; backward-masked (Bb-SDP) 2.437; neuron forward-masked (N-SDP) 2.434. Theorem 3.1: backward masking with single-step SGD is unbiased; its variance grows with the distance of the mask-averaging operator from uniform averaging. At low coverage the regimes separate: on CIFAR-10 at C = 3/8, backward masking holds 87.91 while forward masking falls to 22.56 and 42.68 (Table 4). - **How it differs from us:** H = 1; FLOP-matched rather than step-matched; dense; fixed masks.
- **A question it raises for us:** Would our partial arm's cost vanish at H = 1, as backward-masking theory suggests? That would place the +1.4% in the interaction between partial updates and local steps. (Our harness at H = 1 with a fresh AdamW is not the same as data parallel; see section 6.)

**Which of our choices are known to hurt, and by how much.** Each item below is measured in some paper. Their sizes are not additive and none is measured in our setting (inference, 01).

1. **The baseline.** Our centralized arm takes 4× more sequential steps at the same batch. Most DiLoCo-family papers compare against data parallel at the same number of steps and a larger batch. DiLoCo's Table 2 has both: the more-steps run beats 8×-batch data parallel by 1.44% in loss (inference), and its §5 attributes DiLoCo's cost to outer updates with "effectively too large a batch size". McCandlish et al. give the trade-off: steps S and examples E satisfy (S/S_min − 1)(E/E_min − 1) = 1, with critical batch B_crit = E_min/S_min; at B = B_crit training needs twice the minimum steps and twice the minimum examples ([1812.06162](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06162), Eqs. 2.11-2.12, v1). The critical batch "scales primarily with data size rather than model size" ([2410.21676](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21676), abstract, v4). Our small data budget would push it down, which would make a batch-128 run more likely to lose to batch-32 sequential training (inference, 01). Woodworth et al. treat minibatch SGD with R steps of batch KM as the fair IID comparison for local SGD, and also discuss algorithms that take all MKR stochastic gradients sequentially ([2002.07839](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07839), §2 and §5, v2). Our centralized arm is the latter, the strongest of the baselines they discuss (inference, 01). **Part of our +6.1% may therefore be the price of 4× fewer sequential steps, which no remedy measured against a same-step baseline addresses** (inference). Splitting it needs a batch-128 baseline (section 6).
2. **Plain averaging (outer SGD at lr 1, no momentum).** At 150M with 4 workers and H = 50, plain averaging reaches perplexity 17.75 against 16.38 with Nesterov at outer lr 1.0, about 2.9% in loss ([Khaled et al., Table 3, v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10439); inference). At 178M with 4 workers and H = 30, outer SGD reaches loss 2.937 against 2.911 with Nesterov and 2.881 for data parallel ([Horoi et al., Table 2, v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03011)). DiLoCo found outer SGD "performed poorly" (Fig. 6, figure only), as did Asynchronous Local-SGD at 20M from a pretrained start (§4, figure only; [2401.09135](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09135)). Slow momentum improved Local Adam on WMT translation from validation NLL 2.179 to 2.137, against 2.108 for synchronous Adam ([SlowMo, Table B.1, v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00643)). The best plain outer step can be larger than 1: tuned to 1.6 at 8 workers ([Horoi et al., Table 5, v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03011)); Khaled et al.'s theory says it "should sometimes be set to values greater than 1" (abstract), though their own outer-SGD sweep with a cosine schedule was best at lr 0.5 and tested nothing above 1 (Table 4, v2); FedOpt notes server lr 1 "does not necessarily minimize the upper bound" ([2003.00295](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00295), Remark 1, v5). A reason given in several papers: workers' outer gradients are nearly orthogonal, so averaging shrinks their norm by about 1/√M (DiLoCo §6.2; Decoupled DiLoCo App. D.2, [2604.21428](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21428); Khaled A.1.3 measures cosine similarity, for momentum-based outer optimizers, falling from 30% at 2 workers to 10% at 16 even with IID data). Single-worker DiLoCo also beats plain AdamW (scaling laws, Finding 2; Defazio et al., [2512.17131](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17131), "speedups of up to 6.32%" in steps, §1), so part of tuned DiLoCo's parity with data parallel is an optimizer gain that plain averaging gives up.
3. **A fresh AdamW every round.** The direct evidence is mixed. At a 16-step sync period, resetting raised perplexity from 12.77 to 26.77; at 256 steps it made no difference, 17.79 against 17.72 ([DES-LOC, Fig. 5 legend, v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22549); means over the last round, §4.1). In SlowMo's translation runs, resetting the Adam buffers gave validation loss 4.732 against 2.106 when they were maintained, and the authors write that "reseting the second moment to zeros hurts" (Table B.3, App. B.4, v2). DiLoCo found that synchronizing the states across workers "did not lead to significant improvements" (Supp. §6.1). FoMoE says preserving local momentum "prevents the divergence observed in stateless approaches" but gives no numbers (§3.4, v2). Photon resets the states every round at billion scale and reports good results ([2411.02908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02908), App. A, v2), against a centralized baseline that is not equal-compute in our sense (inference, 01; section 7). Inference (01): our fresh AdamW also restarts the bias correction, and with β2 = 0.95 the second-moment estimate has a time constant of about 20 steps, short against H = 125; the first steps after a reset are close to sign updates of about the learning rate per coordinate. Given the coordinator's correction, those steps run at the full 1e-3 for the first 80% of training. That could produce a loss spike after each reset. It is not measured; our logs could show it (section 6).
4. **A small model.** With 4 workers, tuned DiLoCo's gap falls from +2.0% at 35M to +1.5% at 90M and +0.3% at 2.4B ([scaling laws, Table 4, v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.09799)). A federated-pretraining paper reports that centralized training "outperforms the 75M federated model while performing near-identically for the 1.3B models" ([2405.10853](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10853), Fig. 3 caption, v3; figure only). Photon reports "perplexity gains grow with model size" (§5.2). DEPT, at its smallest body (86M), finds plain averaging slightly worse than its baseline and writes that aggregation "may be overly harsh" at that scale ([2410.05021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05021), Table 13 and App. B.7, v5). Extrapolating the scaling-law trend down to our ~14.2M active parameters would predict a gap larger than at 35M (inference, 01). This has not been measured.
5. **H = 125 over 40 rounds.** Every language-model study with an H sweep finds the gap grows with H beyond very small H: at 178M with 8 workers, loss 2.950, 2.957, 2.976 and 3.008 at H = 30, 60, 120 and 240, that is +2.4% to +4.4% against data parallel ([Horoi et al., Table 4, v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03011); inference); at 35M with 4 workers, +2.6% at H = 30 and +3.7% at H = 100 ([Streaming DiLoCo, Tables 5 and 6, v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18512); inference); at 1.1B with 4 workers, perplexity 10.76 at H = 125 against 11.14 at H = 500 and 10.52 for data parallel ([OpenDiLoCo, Table 2, v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07852)); at 1.5B with 8 workers, 18.65, 22.67 and 28.17 at H = 100, 500 and 1,000 against 17.53, which is +2.2%, +9.0% and +16.6% with about 190, 38 and 19 rounds ([Factored Gossip DiLoCo, §5-§6, v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22768); inference). SPES finds the same direction at 1B (Fig. A2, figure only). The exception: plain averaging in Khaled et al. is "relatively constant" from H = 250 to 2,000, already about as bad as one worker at a quarter of the compute. Streaming DiLoCo states the trade-off: too small an H gives "noisy small-normed outer gradients", too large an H lets "replicas drift apart" (Supplement). In every sweep, H and the number of rounds move together at fixed total steps, so the two cannot be separated; the very large gaps occur with few rounds (inference, 01).
6. **The learning-rate schedule.** DiLoCo found the inner-lr decay already shrinks the outer gradients, so decaying the outer lr was unnecessary (§3.1). Khaled et al. found a constant outer lr best (A.1.5). FedOpt shows that the client lr, not the server lr, must decay for drift to vanish, because client drift "does not vanish as T → ∞ when ηl is constant" (Discussion ii, v5). The Quadratic Synchronization Rule sets H in proportion to 1/η² as the lr decays, and improves ImageNet accuracy over data parallel ([2310.14423](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14423), abstract and §1 only, v2; vision). INTELLECT-1 used a warmup-stable-decay schedule with a final-20% cool-down at 10B, but with no controlled baseline ([2412.01152](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01152), §3.1, v1). Our lr stays at 1e-3 for 80% of training, H does not change during the decay, and the decay ends at 1e-4. No paper isolates a warmup-stable-decay inner schedule under local averaging (not found in this search).
7. **IID data.** SCAFFOLD's client drift needs heterogeneous clients; with identical data FedAvg's bound has no drift term, but local steps then help only the noise term, not the optimization term ([1910.06378](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06378), §3.1, v4). So control-variate methods target a cause our IID setup lacks (inference, 01). Woodworth et al. show that for non-quadratic objectives the error of local SGD depends on the local steps and the rounds separately, not just their product (§2-§3, v2). The theory predicts the direction of our cost, larger with H at fixed total steps, but nothing in it predicts 6% for a 46M MoE (inference, 01).
8. **The MoE itself.** The only evidence on MoEs under local averaging is Decoupled DiLoCo at 2.8B and 3.8B active parameters: learners "can only optimize [the load-balancing] loss locally", yet "with sufficient scale" match data parallel (§5.5; downstream accuracies only). Nothing below 2.8B active was found. FoMoE's full-replica runs are local averaging of a 54M MoE but have no centralized number.

**Where the literature disagrees.**

- **Plain averaging against Nesterov.** Photon, in the configuration structurally closest to ours (125M, 4 clients, local batch 32, global batch 128), reports plain averaging reaching its target perplexity in about half of DiLoCo's wall time (Table 4, v2), because DiLoCo's outer lr above 0.1 "hindered achieving the desired perplexity due to early divergent activations" (§5.3, Fig. 6). FoMoE's Nesterov runs degrade badly at K = 128 (Tables 4 and 5, v2), and in Khaled et al. Nesterov at lr 1.0 is worse than plain averaging at H = 1,000 and 2,000. DiLoCo, Khaled et al. at small H, and Horoi et al. find Nesterov better. Inference (01): Nesterov can be unstable at small batch and small scale and needs its lr retuned with H; this does not show plain averaging is better when Nesterov is stable.
- **Model size and H.** The scaling laws find larger models tolerate local steps better; Factored Gossip finds tolerance of H = 1,000 falls from 160M to 1.5B (Fig. 5, figure only), with a fixed outer lr and few rounds.
- **Optimizer reset.** Photon uses it; SlowMo and DES-LOC find it harmful at short sync periods.
- **Whether local averaging costs anything.** DEPT and DiPaCo (§4.5) find local updates match or beat synchronous training under their baselines, which are matched in sequential steps (DEPT, with a lowered baseline lr) or start from a pretrained model (DiPaCo §4.5). DiPaCo's Table 1 and Stage 0, with equal-compute baselines, find a cost.

**What is unknown (not found in this search):** local averaging of character-level models; local averaging of MoEs below 2.8B active; an Adam reset at H of about 100 to 150 with β2 = 0.95; a warmup-stable-decay inner schedule under local averaging; the cost of a more-steps baseline measured with 4 workers; any study combining a reset, plain averaging and a small model; and therefore how our 6.1 points divide among the handicaps above.

### 3.2 Question 2: the masked-routing cost (+3.2%) and interchangeable experts

**How experts come to specialize.** The papers describe a co-adaptation between router, experts and trunk:

- In Chen et al.'s theory the router starts nearly uniform, each expert drifts toward the cluster its random initialization favours, and the router then learns the cluster centres; if the router errs early, "the experts may amplify the mistake" ([2208.02813](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02813), §1 and §5, v1; theorems not read). In DDOME's linear-Gaussian theory, training experts under a gate frozen at initialization and training the gate afterwards leaves the expected loss bounded below by a constant: joint gate-expert training is needed ([2306.08586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08586), Theorem 1, v3).
- In X-MoE's analysis the gradient through the gate pulls each token's representation toward the router row it was sent to, so router rows and trunk representations shape each other, and representations tend to collapse into a low-dimensional subspace ([2204.09179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09179), §2.2, Eqs. 4-6, v3).
- Routing settles early. In OLMoE up to 60% of top-8 routing already matches the final checkpoint after 1% of pretraining, and layer 0 settles "significantly more slowly" ([2409.02060](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02060), §5.1, v2). Changing the balance scope late recovers only part of the gain because router choices are "fixed early" (Demons, Table 3). Random routing perturbations are harmless around 1K steps but cause loss spikes later ([Grouter, 2603.06626](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.06626), §2.2, figure only). Even so, a learned-routing baseline in StableMoE still changed the expert of 15.4% of tokens after 80% of training (Fig. 2), and freezing a distilled router after 10% of training improved perplexity by 0.63 ([2204.08396](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08396), Table 5, v1).
- Routing knowledge can live in the trunk. A router frozen at random initialization matched a learned one at GPT-2-small scale (Fan et al., Table 3) and in a T5-Base MoE (70.34% against 70.27% pretraining accuracy), where the learned router won only after the model width was cut to 32 (Dikkala et al., EMNLP 2023, DOI [10.18653/v1/2023.emnlp-main.583](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.583.pdf), §3.7 and appendix; see section 7).

What experts specialize in: token types and syntax more than topics. ST-MoE's encoder experts specialize in punctuation, verbs, names and numbers, while its decoder routing is near uniform ([2202.08906](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.08906), Tables 13-14, v2). Mixtral shows syntax and temporal locality but no topic structure ([2401.04088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04088), §5, v1). Fan et al. find no topic specialization with token-level routing. Domain specialization appears when the balance loss is computed over a large, varied batch (OLMoE §5.3; Demons §4.2). No study of what character-level experts learn was found.

Inference (02a): in our sliced arm, router row j learns only on workers holding expert j, while the trunk is averaged over all four workers and each worker's trunk is pulled toward the rows of its own four experts. Each round restarts from merged weights with a fresh optimizer. The consistent input-to-expert mapping that Fan et al. and Dikkala et al. find sufficient has little chance to form, and the symmetry-breaking Chen et al. describe may be reset every round.

**What drives experts toward uniformity or redundancy,** roughly by strength of evidence:

1. **Inconsistent routing.** StableMoE's "routing fluctuation" means "the same input updates different experts but only one is finally used" (abstract). MaskMoE argues that dynamic routing disperses a token's occurrences across experts, and finds that giving each token a fixed random subset of visible experts beats routing over all 64 (4 visible: 6.551 against 6.618 perplexity; [2407.09816](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09816), Table 5, v4). In Fan et al., a fixed random mapping keeps the MoE gain while a mapping redrawn every iteration loses most of it (11.752 against 9.810, close to the dense 11.815).
2. **A narrow balance scope.** Balancing per micro-batch makes routing nearly uniform within sequences and suppresses domain specialization (Demons, 3.4B to 43B). ST-MoE gives the same explanation for its lack of language specialization (§7.3). At 86M, weaker balance raised a routing-category mutual information from 0.1148 at coefficient 0.01 to 0.1482 at 0.005 ([2608.15102](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15102), Table 9 and §5.2, v2; one main run per condition). A NeurIPS 2025 paper argues the balance loss sends tokens to experts with no affinity for them and creates a feedback loop toward uniform routing, but its evidence is from short LoRA fine-tuning ([2505.22323](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22323), §2.2, v5).
3. **Shared initialization.** Mixtral's experts have clearly non-zero weight similarity and near-uniform routing by domain; two papers attribute this to how Mixtral was initialized. OLMoE suggests upcycling from one dense model, so that experts "start from the same local optimum" (§5.3); Lo et al. point to "specific initialization schemes" ([2406.18219](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18219), §1 and §4.1, v3).
4. **Deliberately forced or stochastic routing.** Gating Dropout forces local routing to give experts "a general ability" (§3). THOR's consistency loss trains experts toward "making the same predictions as much as possible" ([2110.04260](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.04260), §4, v3).

Collapse in the other direction, all tokens to a few experts, is what the balance loss prevents. It occurred in OLMoE without the balance loss (layer 0 onto one or two experts; §4.1.6), in Fan et al. (where it did not hurt and experts recovered), and in our own Task B (3 to 5 of 8 experts). Softmax followed by top-K "can also encourage expert collapsing" (σ-MoE §5), and softmax gates are "more sensitive to load imbalance" than sigmoid gates ([2408.15664](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15664), App. C, v1).

**Is there direct evidence that training under a restricted or masked router produces generalist or redundant experts? Not found in this search.** No paper trains a learned router that sees only a worker-specific subset of experts for many steps, merges it with routers that saw other subsets, and then measures specialization or redundancy. The search covered 13 discovery queries on specialization, redundancy, collapse, random routing, expert dropout and restricted routing (02a); 12 on federated, decentralized and merged MoE (02b); forward citations of Gating Dropout (34 Semantic Scholar citers), MaskMoE (12), HARC, FedAlign-MoE, DDOME and FoMoE, plus 20 arXiv API queries (06). The nearest evidence:

The pattern across these (inference): restrictions that are consistent for a given input (MaskMoE, MoEC within clusters) or chosen by content (EMO, DeepSeek-V2) are harmless or helpful; restrictions that are random with respect to content cost more as the masked fraction grows (MoEC, Gating Dropout beyond p ≈ 0.3). Our mask is content-independent under IID data, covers half the experts, and differs by worker and by round.

**Federated and merged MoEs: mostly a non-IID story.** The named failure modes, "gating misalignment" and "expert semantic blurring", are demonstrated under strongly non-IID data, in figures ([FedAlign-MoE, 2603.21276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21276), §II, v2). They shrink as data approach IID. FedAlign-MoE's gain over FedAvg on AGNews is +7.8 points at Dirichlet α = 0.1, +3.8 at α = 1.0 and +1.6 under IID, and its IID MMLU gap is still +4.9 (0.4641 against 0.5131) (inference from Tables I-II, v2). Averaged MoEs beat dense models under IID (81.51 against 78.93) and lose under α = 0.1 (50.60 against 55.78) ([FedCoE, 2605.21264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21264), §III, v1). FLEx's advantage over MoE + FedAvg (FLEx freezes the experts and does not average them) is 2.0 ROUGE-L at α = 0.1 and 0.03 at α = 1.0 (inference from [2506.00965](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00965), Table 2, v2). The only expert-subset paper with an IID condition, FLEX-MoE, finds random per-round assignment loses 7 to 11 points (inference from Table I, v2) to fitness-based assignment on CIFAR-10, but has no full-model or centralized baseline ([2512.23070](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.23070), Table I, v2). Inference (02b): blurring needs same-index experts to diverge; with IID data and H = 125 this should be weak, so it probably does not explain Task A's generalists.

**Training apart on IID data produces no specialists.** Branch-Train-Merge's random-split experts lose to the dense model at every scale (Tables 1 and 4). MoE-DisCo's advantage "essentially regresses to" full-parameter training under random data assignment ([2601.06857](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06857), §4.5.1, Fig. 6, v1; figure only). In KALAVAI's fine-tuning study the gain from fusing specialists is about 0.82 × divergence − 2.72 (R² = 0.856, six points) and near zero below about 3.3% divergence ([2603.22755](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22755), §4.10, Table 12, v2). DEPT trained apart on IID C4 shards performs "similarly to standard pre-training" (App. B.3, figure only). Inference (04b): nothing in this literature predicts that experts trained apart on IID shards specialize, which fits Task A. None of these papers measures a masked-routing cost, so none predicts its size.

**Candidate mechanisms for our sliced arm** (all inference from the notes; none tested):

- **Routing share.** On a sliced worker, top-2 over 4 held experts sends each held expert about half of the tokens, against about a quarter centrally. Each held expert trains on every input type, alongside a different set of companions each round, and its 1 to 4 copies are then averaged. This needs no balance loss, so it can act in Task A, which ran without one (02a). It also fits the comparison arms: the partial arm, which routes over all 8, kept most of the specialization (02a).
- **Balance scope (Task B only).** Each worker computes the balance loss over its own batch and its 4 held experts, pushing them toward equal shares: a narrow scope, as in Demons. EMO found micro-batch balancing "directly opposing the shared-pool constraint" of restricted pools and needed global balance (§3.3, v2).
- **Softmax renormalization over held experts.** FoMoE uses sigmoid gating so a missing expert does not "skew the probability distribution" of the others (App. B.3.2). Ours renormalizes over the chosen 2 of 4 during local training and over 2 of 8 after the merge. No ablation exists.
- **Uncompared expert pairs.** Within a round, the router compares two experts only on workers holding both. FlexOlmo needed a bias because its model "never directly compares" separately trained experts (§3.3.2). Because coverage redraws every round, which pairs go uncompared changes from round to round. (Correction to 02b, which assumed a fixed assignment.)
- **Forward-masking bias.** Masked routing removes experts from the forward pass. Theory for sub-model gradients finds a bias and an "irreducible neighborhood" that a smaller step size does not remove ([Shulgin and Richtárik, 2306.16484](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16484), §4.2 and App. C.2, v2), and SDP's forward-masked runs collapse at low coverage where backward-masked ones do not (Table 4). The partial arm is backward-masked (inference, 03).
- **Router merging.** HARC's mechanism (softmax of averaged logits, then top-k) applies whenever router rows are averaged; for us the larger change is structural, from top-2 over 4 to top-2 over 8 (inference, 02b). Routing Drift finds most route changes after merging come from shifted inputs; in our arm the trunk, averaged over all workers, is one such input shift (inference, 06).

**Does random or hash routing work nearly as well?** It depends on what "random" means:

Scale and expert count matter. The benefit of any routing shrinks as the dense base grows from 15M to 1.3B, but learned routing stays ahead of hashing at every size tested (Clark et al., §4.2, §5.3). Hashing gains on learned routing as experts are added (Hash Layers, Fig. 3), and the authors suggest "learning to route" matters more with few experts. With 27 characters, a token hash would give each of 8 experts 3 or 4 characters and no context, so hash results do not transfer directly (inference, 02a). Our Task A probe is the third notion applied at inference: the centralized model lost about half its accuracy, far more than the published probes on large learned-routing models, and the sliced model lost almost nothing (inference, 02a).

At our size the payoff from specialization may be small. MoEs did not beat dense models at 10M to 20M active parameters (Slicing and Dicing, §3.1), a σ-MoE matched but did not beat a dense model of equal total size at 41M on enwik8 (Table 3), and in SMoE-Dropout a learned SMoE trailed an equal-parameter dense model by 2.35% on enwik8 (inference from Table 1, 05). SMoE-Dropout's width study (Fig. 5b) is unresolved and not used (section 7).

**Where the literature disagrees.** Shared experts help (DeepSeekMoE at 2B, [2401.06066](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06066), §4.4; MaskMoE, 6.51 to 6.48) or slightly hurt (OLMoE §4.1.3; Slicing and Dicing §3.2). Collapse without a balance loss did not hurt at GPT-2-small scale (Fan et al.) but did at about 1B and above (OLMoE Fig. 9; aux-loss-free Fig. 2). Global balance helped at 3.4B to 43B (Demons), while ST-MoE reports that a global balance loss gave "worse load-balance and worse model performance" (§7.3, no numbers). Hash routing beats learned routing in some settings and loses in others (above). The balance coefficient 0.01 is the Switch and OLMoE default but the largest value in the aux-loss-free sweep (§2.2, Fig. 2), whose sigmoid baseline used 1e-3 as a trade-off between quality and balance (§4.1) and whose best softmax-gate coefficient was 3e-4 (App. C.2); a weak 1e-4 without a bias was the worst setting in Slicing and Dicing. HARC finds router recalibration helps after merging; Routing Drift finds route changes do not diagnose failure and HARC's gains are small and of either sign.

**What is unknown (not found in this search):** any measurement of specialization or redundancy after training under a worker-specific restricted router with averaging; router saturation or fluctuation under periodic averaging; z-loss's effect on specialization or restricted routing; what character-level experts specialize in; learned against frozen against hash routing at 10M to 50M on characters with 8 experts; the size of the MoE gain over dense at our budget; and any separation of data heterogeneity from structural heterogeneity, that is IID data with different held subsets, which is our case.

### 3.3 Question 3: the partial-update cost (+1.4%)

**Our number, restated (03).** The step is +1.4 points of centralized bits per character (+1.3 to +1.6 over seeds). Measured directly against full averaging it is +1.37% (mean of per-seed ratios), or +0.021 bits per character (inference, 03). In this arm each worker runs the full forward pass over all 8 experts, updates its 4, and the router is averaged over all workers. With the coverage policy an expert has 1 to 4 holders in a round (29.6%, 44.5%, 22.2%, 3.7%). Each worker trains about 54% of the parameters: the shared 3.58M plus 4 of 8 experts, that is 21.2M of the 42.47M expert parameters (inference; experts are SwiGLU with three matrices and hold 92% of the 46.05M).

By trainable fraction (≈54%) we match Filippova's 1/4 row (≈52%, −0.1%), and by holder share (1/2) their 1/2 row (−1.6%). Both are at or below zero, so our +1.4% is above them. By holder count (about 2) we match their 1/16 row (+4.3%), so our cost is below it. Against Ro et al.'s 40% row we are inside or slightly above the range while training a larger fraction (inference, 03). **A cost of 1 to 2% for partial updates of a few tens of percent of parameters is consistent with the language-model literature**, and it can vanish or turn negative with many holders, an outer optimizer, or extra compute. Its sign and size are not robust across settings.

**What theory says.**

- **Partial updates after a full forward pass are unbiased per step and pay only variance.** In SDP's Theorem 3.1 the variance term grows with the distance of the mask-averaging operator from uniform averaging, so fewer holders mean more variance. With no forward perturbation, the alignment and similarity conditions of partial SGD hold automatically, and convergence depends only on how much gradient norm the mask keeps ([Mohtashami et al., 2106.08895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08895), §3.3, v3). SPES's own bound gives the shared block a 1/N variance reduction and the expert blocks none (App. A).
- **Coverage matters under IID data.** For training with extracted sub-models and holder averaging, the gap to a stationary point shrinks as the minimum number of holders rises, and "increasing local epochs or communication rounds cannot mitigate such noise" ([Every Parameter Matters, 2310.08670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08670), Theorem 1 and §6.2, v2). Its bias term comes from forward masking, which our partial arm does not do (inference, 03). Our per-round minimum holder count is 1 under coverage and would be 2 under rolling.
- **Sub-model gradients (forward masking) are biased.** Independent subnetwork training converges to a neighbourhood that a smaller step size cannot remove (Shulgin and Richtárik, Theorem 2, App. C.2, v2). This maps onto our sliced arm, not the partial arm (inference, 03).
- **None of these analyses covers many local steps.** In our partial arm, a worker's frozen experts stay fixed while its shared part and router move for 125 steps. That staleness is not analysed anywhere (inference, 03).

Inference (03): for our partial arm the theory points to variance, from averaging an expert over about 2 trajectories, sometimes 1, and from each expert getting gradients from only its holders' data, rather than to bias.

**Dependence on fraction, holders, workers, H and assignment.**

- **Fraction updated:** monotone within each paper: Ro et al. (100% better than 40% better than 20%), Filippova (more slices, worse), SDP on CIFAR (accuracy falls from coverage 7/8 to 3/8), IST (more nodes and smaller subnets, lower accuracy; [1910.02120](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02120), Table 3, v7). Filippova shows the curve need not start at zero: mild slicing improved perplexity.
- **Extra compute closes the gap:** Filippova's 1/8 slicing "required almost 50% more tokens to match" the smaller-slice runs; at 37B tokens it reached 12.68, against 12.75 for full training at 26B; and SDP's sub-networks match or beat data parallel at 1B when given FLOP-matched extra steps.
- **Worker count:** no paper varies it at fixed total compute and fixed holder share for partial updates.
- **Local steps:** no paper measures how the partial-against-full gap changes with H at matched compute. SDP (H = 1, about −0.6%), Filippova (H = 100, −1.6% to +4.3%) and ours (H = 125, +1.4%) hint that H matters, but they differ in everything else (inference, 03).
- **Assignment:** rolling beat static nested beat random sub-models on a Transformer LM (FedRolex, forward-masked); new random masks per client per round beat one mask per round, which "is unable to converge to reasonable WERs within 200K rounds" (PVT §3.4.1); re-sampling subnets beat fixed partitions for deeper networks (IST Table 3); fixed overlapping slices work at 1.3B and 1B (Filippova, SDP). FedRolex's penalty comes from nested capacities in which some parameters are rarely trained; our coverage policy trains every expert every round, so that penalty probably does not transfer (inference, 03). Filippova did not try reassignment because optimizer state would have to move; our fresh AdamW each round removes that cost (inference, 03).

**SPES** is our partial arm at scale and reports no cost (section 3.4). Its result does not test ours: a coarse benchmark metric, H = 50, AdamW kept, disjoint ownership, and a warm-up it needs to stay above centralized.

**Where the literature disagrees.** Partial training sometimes beats full training: Filippova's 1/2 slicing, SDP at 500M and 1B, IST's Table 2, and Federated Dropout ("matches or, in some cases, even improves", [1812.07210](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.07210), §4.3, v2). Elsewhere it consistently costs: Ro et al., ResIST, FedRolex, HeteroFL, InclusiveFL. The papers offer FLOP matching (SDP), regularization or ensemble effects (SDP §4.2; Federated Dropout) and the outer optimizer and scale as explanations; Filippova does not explain its improvement. Within SDP alone the same backward-masked regime costs nothing at 1B (coverage 0.69) and 3.7 and 8.7 accuracy points (inference from Table 4) on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 at coverage 4/8, even with extra steps.

**What is unknown (not found in this search):** any comparison of partial against full expert updates in an MoE under the same local-averaging scheme, other than SPES's coarse one; the dependence of the partial cost on H; the effect of holder count separately from trainable fraction; worker count at fixed compute and holder share; any character-level result with a full-model baseline (FjORD's character-level Shakespeare LSTM, [2102.13451](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.13451), has none in the sections read).

### 3.4 Question 4: the closest prior methods

**SPES, read in full** ([2602.11543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11543), v3, with v1 and v2 diffed, and its code; 04a). Setting: MoE pretraining from scratch at 1.1B (controlled comparison), 2B and 7B, plus a 9B model upcycled from a dense checkpoint; IID data per node; disjoint expert ownership; each node stores the full model. H = 50 for the 1B comparison and 100 for the larger runs; for the 2B and 7B runs the per-node batch is halved and H set to 50 after 70% of tokens (App. B). Routing is not masked: tokens go to all experts, including frozen local copies of experts a node does not own; the router trains on every node and is averaged over all nodes (code). AdamW state and the cosine schedule continue across rounds (code). ### 3.5 Question 5: remedies with evidence

Section 4 ranks the remedies. The overall shape of the evidence:

- **Remedies for the averaging cost have the strongest evidence**: outer momentum or a tuned outer step, and a smaller H, are each measured in several language-model pretraining papers between 35M and 1.5B. Even so, every one of those papers keeps the inner optimizer state and compares against a same-step baseline, so their effect sizes are not directly ours.
- **Inner-optimizer state handling has direct but mixed evidence**: a reset costs a great deal at short sync periods and nothing at long ones, and no measurement sits at our H and β2.
- **Remedies for the partial-update cost** (more holders, larger slices, extra compute, assignment policy) are measured in dense pretraining at 1B to 1.3B, in a small federated LM, and for the combined partial and masked cost in FoMoE.
- **Remedies for the masked-routing cost** are measured almost only in centralized training, in one-shot merges of fine-tuned models, or in federated fine-tuning, mostly at billions of parameters. After compression, a router-only distillation improved a 128-expert MoE, while the gains were "relatively marginal for Mixtral", which has 8 experts like ours ([Router KD, 2603.02217](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02217), §5.4, prose).
- **No measured evidence was found** for routing a fraction of tokens to non-held experts with remote computation during local steps, or for knowledge distillation in iterative MoE pretraining.

### 3.6 Question 6: the sanity of our baseline

**Is about 1.44 bits per character plausible?** Probably, for our budget and protocol; there is no published run to check it against (05).

- Every headline (best-configuration) autoregressive transformer result on text8 found lies between 1.07 (adaptive span, [1905.07799](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07799), Table 1, v2) and 1.29 (an SMoE with k = 2 in S2MoE, [2503.23007](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23007), Table 1, v1) bits per character. Every one with a stated training budget (Transformer-XL's is not stated; section 7) used 15 to 800 times our 0.164B training characters, that is 27 to about 1,456 passes over the data against our 1.8 (inference, 05).
- Most were scored with 512 or more characters of context per prediction (stride-1 sliding windows, recurrent memory, or 32k windows).
- The one transformer scored in 256-character chunks, as we do, is D3PM's 12-layer, ~70M decoder: **1.23** after about 1,456 passes ([2107.03006](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03006), Table 1, v3; passes are an inference from App. B.2; whether its chunks overlap is not stated, section 7).
- Plain recurrent models reach 1.43 (a 2,000-unit LSTM; [1603.09025](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09025), Table 3, v5; parameter count not stated) and 1.40 (an unregularized mLSTM; [1609.07959](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.07959), Table 2, v3). Both were early-stopped; their training length and test-time state handling are not stated (section 7).
- No text8 or enwik8 result at about 2 passes was found (not found in this search).

Inference (05; the parameter ratio uses the header's corrected count): our 1.4376 is 0.21 bits per character (16.9%) above D3PM's chunk-length-matched 1.23 (overlap not stated; section 7), from a model with about 5× our active parameters and 800× our data. That gap is unsurprising but cannot be predicted. Our baseline is **not unusually strong**; it is weaker than the converged literature mostly because of budget and evaluation protocol, and nothing read points to a defect. Nothing read rules out a few hundredths of a bit of hyperparameter slack either.

**How much does non-overlapping 256-character scoring cost?** No direct measurement on text8 or enwik8 was found. At word level, Shortformer found non-overlapping scoring at length 256 raises perplexity from 18.86 to 21.45, 4.38% in loss ([2012.15832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15832), Table 1, v2; inference, 05); 256 words hold far more text than 256 characters, so this size does not transfer. From Al-Rfou et al.'s context table, assuming loss falls with context, a lower bound for a model of T64's quality is 1.205 bits per character, at least +0.045 (+3.9%) over stride-1 scoring at context 256 and +0.075 (+6.6%) over context 512 (inference, 05). All our arms were scored the same way. If the penalty were the same absolute amount in every arm, sliding-window scoring would lower the denominator and raise our relative gaps slightly, for example +6.1% to about 6.3% to 6.5% (inference, 05). Whether the penalty is equal across arms is untested.

**Is a 5% allowance large or small?** 5% of 1.4376 is 0.072 bits per character (inference, 05). By converged text8 and enwik8 standards it is generous, about the gain from a 5× to 7× larger model or one architecture generation at the same size (inference, 05):

|---|---|---|
| T12 (41M) to T64 (219M), text8 (Al-Rfou, Table 1, v2) | 1.18 to 1.13 | 4.2% |
| Transformer-XL 41M to 277M, enwik8 ([1901.02860](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.02860), Table 2, v3) | 1.06 to 0.99 | 6.6% |

Relative changes are 05's arithmetic (inference). At 1.8 passes, though, bits per character are still falling steeply with data (a 15M model was not saturated after 130B tokens: Clark et al., App. F), so 0.072 bits may correspond to a much smaller compute factor than in converged tables (inference, 05). Against distributed methods, tuned local averaging stays under 5% at 35M with 4 workers (+2.0%) and 8 workers (+3.9%) (scaling laws, Table 4), and our plain arm does not. The criterion is neither impossibly strict nor lax (inference, 05).

**Does an MoE help at about 14M active parameters?** The evidence points to little or no gain at our budget. Clark et al. found routing helped down to a 15M dense base, but at 130B tokens on subwords ([2202.01169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01169), §1, §2, App. A, v2). At 41M on enwik8 (about 18 passes, inference) a 25%-active MoE matched a dense model of the same total size (σ-MoE, Table 3). In SMoE-Dropout's 4-layer enwik8 model, a learned SMoE trailed an equal-parameter dense model by 2.35% (inference from Table 1, 05). At 10M to 20M active, MoEs did not beat dense at default budgets (Slicing and Dicing). ST-MoE warns that small-scale MoE findings often "vanish, or even reverse" at larger scale or longer training (§9). Whether our MoE beats a dense model at 1.8 passes needs a dense run (section 6).

## 6. Gaps

These are questions no paper read answers and our harness could answer. The harness options named are those listed in the brief: other H, Nesterov outer momentum, slice sizes, worker counts, random, coverage and rolling assignment, sliced, partial and full modes, skewed data and worker dropout, plus the Task A random-routing and expert-removal probes. Brackets give the notes that raised each gap. Nothing here is a plan; it is a list of open questions.

**A. New runs with existing options**

1. **The averaging cost as a function of H, with and without an outer optimizer.** Full mode, 4 workers, fixed total compute, for example H = 25, 50, 125, 250, 500 (R = 200, 100, 40, 20, 10), plain averaging against Nesterov. No character-level or ~14M-active H curve exists. Options: other H, Nesterov outer momentum, full mode. Caveat: H = 1 with a fresh AdamW every round is not data parallel at batch 128, because each step would be a bias-corrected first Adam step, close to a sign update (inference, 01). [01 G1; 02a 2; 02b 2; 03 G3; 04a 2; 04b 1]
2. **An outer learning-rate sweep at H = 125**: several Nesterov lr values, and lr above 1 without momentum (Horoi et al. tuned plain outer SGD to lr 1.6; Khaled et al.'s theory says it should sometimes exceed 1, though their own SGD sweep was best at 0.5). Option: Nesterov outer momentum; whether the harness accepts an outer lr above 1 is to be checked in the code (01). [01 G2]
3. **Nesterov outer momentum in all three arms** (full, partial, sliced) at H = 125, including momentum applied per expert over its holders: does it shrink the partial and masked shares as well as the averaging share? Options: Nesterov outer momentum; sliced, partial and full modes. [02b 7; 03 G4; 04a 3; 04b 2]
4. **Worker count at fixed total compute**: 2, 4 and 8 workers in full and sliced modes, the bits-per-character analogue of SPES's Table A5. Published worker-count curves come from subword models of 35M and above: dense in the DiLoCo family, MoE in SPES (1B, Table A5) and FoMoE (54M, Tables 3-6). Option: worker counts. [01 G3; 04a 5; 04b 4]
5. **Holder count separated from trainable fraction, and a masking dose-response.** For example 8 workers with 4 of 8 experts (fraction about 54%, about 4 holders), 4 workers with 2 (about 1 holder), 4 workers with 6 (about 3 holders) (03). Sliced runs at 2 to 7 of 8 experts, each paired with a partial run at the same slice size, would give the masking share at each size: the analogue of Gating Dropout's p and MoEC's mask fraction. Holder counts 1, 2 and 4 would replicate FoMoE's Table 6 with the centralized anchor FoMoE lacks. Options: slice sizes, worker counts, sliced and partial modes. [02a 1; 02b 3, 5; 03 G1; 04a 1; 04b 1; 06]
6. **Assignment policy**: coverage (1 to 4 holders per round) against rolling (exactly 2) against random, measured on loss and on the interchangeability probes. This tests the holder-count variance that SDP's Theorem 3.1 and Every Parameter Matters point to. A fixed (pinned) assignment, or giving every worker the same subset in a round (the MaskMoE consistency contrast), only if an assignment policy can express it. Options: random, coverage and rolling assignment. [02a 5; 02b 4; 03 G2; 04b 5]
7. **Disjoint against overlapping experts in the partial arm**, SPES's assignment, if a slice size and policy combination gives each expert exactly one holder. Options: slice sizes, assignment, partial mode. [04a 4]
8. **Skewed against IID data in all three arms.** Does the masked-routing cost grow or shrink with skew, or is it structural? Do content-correlated worker data restore specialization, as EMO's content-chosen pools suggest (inference, 04a)? Does the partial cost grow, as Every Parameter Matters predicts once coverage stops helping? Does averaging acquire the drift term that IID theory says is absent (01)? Option: skewed data. [01 G7; 02a 6; 02b 1; 03 G7; 04a 6; 04b 3]
9. **Heterogeneous per-worker slice sizes**, for example 2, 4, 4 and 6 of 8. No paper measured this in language-model pretraining. Option: slice sizes (configurable per worker, S0-F-05). [04a 7]
10. **Worker dropout**: experts that temporarily have one holder, and whether dropout acts as a regularizer, as Learning@home claims from a figure. Option: worker dropout. [02b 8; 04b 6]
11. **Keeping against resetting AdamW at H = 125** (and, as a third arm, averaging its moments), in the full-averaging arm and then the partial and sliced arms. DES-LOC brackets our H, with a large cost at a 16-step period and none at 256; SPES's code and FoMoE keep the state, we reset it. The brief lists this among questions the harness could answer; note 01 judges that a small code change is needed, since keeping state is not among the listed options. To be checked in the code. [01 G5; 04a; 04b]

**B. Analyses of existing logs and saved models (no new training)**

12. **Per-round loss traces around each merge and each AdamW reset**, to see whether reset-induced spikes, as DES-LOC reports, occur at H = 125 and β2 = 0.95. [01 G6]
13. **Sliding-window re-scoring of the saved Task B models**, giving every character a full 256-character context (a window advanced by 1 or 32 characters), and loss by position inside the window. This measures our protocol penalty in every arm and shows whether the +6.1%, +7.6% and +10.8% gaps change. Needs an evaluation change, not retraining. [05 1, 2]
14. **An equivalent-compute reading of each gap**: the step at which the centralized validation curve reaches each arm's final bits per character, with the caveat that validation and test sets differ. [05 3]
15. **Task B interchangeability probes in all four arms**: random routing, single-expert removal, one layer randomized at a time (comparable to UniPool), and a "disable the top-k routed experts" curve (DeepSeekMoE). Uses the Task A probes. [02a 3]
16. **Per-expert diagnosis**: each expert's cumulative holder count and token count against its final usage and quality. [03 G6]
17. **Routing diagnostics suggested by the papers**: router saturation and the share of characters changing top-1 expert between rounds (OLMoE, StableMoE); the cosine of same-index expert updates across holders (FedAlign-MoE); Jensen-Shannon divergence between workers' routing (MedMix); the share of local top-2 choices over 4 that survive the merged top-2 over 8 (HARC); the cosine between router rows (FlexOlmo §5.1); and crossing trunk and router to separate input-induced from parameter-induced route changes (Routing Drift). Some need only a small logging hook (02b). [02a 4; 02b 6; 06]

**C. Baselines through the centralized script** (likely configuration changes, to be verified in the script; 01)

18. **Centralized at batch 128 for 5,000 steps**, the same-step data-parallel analogue, and **at batch 32 for 5,000 steps**, the one-worker floor. These split the +6.1% into "fewer sequential steps" and "local averaging itself". Khaled et al.'s plain averaging at H ≥ 250 was no better than such a floor (inference, 01). [01 G4]
19. **A longer centralized run** (2× or 4× the steps): the slope of bits per character against data at our scale, and so a data-equivalent reading of the 0.072-bit allowance. [05 5]
20. **A compute-matched partial arm**: give it the extra steps its skipped expert gradients would buy (SDP's accounting) to test whether more training closes the gap, as with Filippova's 1/8 slicing. Whether this is only a configuration change is to be checked. [03 G5]
